F1 Race Strategy from Telemetry

At the 2026 Italian Grand Prix, Kimi Antonelli pitted under the Virtual Safety Car on lap 28, came out on new mediums and passed his Mercedes teammate George Russell on lap 50 to win. I am building a counterfactual race model in Python to answer one question: if Russell had stopped too, would he have won?

Summary

A data project that treats a strategy call as a hypothesis to test. I measure each part of the answer from real timing data, combine the parts in a lap-by-lap race simulation, and wrote down the rejection criterion before measuring anything.

Engineering problem

One "what if" depends on several uncertain parts at once: the cost of a stop under VSC, which tyres were left, what the second car loses in a double stack, and whether a faster car can pass at Monza.

Tools

Python, FastF1, pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, Streamlit (my own app to explore laps, gaps and channels)

Findings so far

Russell had no new mediums on lap 28; the set was used in Q1. VSC stop loss measured at about 13.7 s. Overtaking rule calibrated to reproduce Antonelli's real race. Verdict pending.

The stint behind the question

Under the red flag after Leclerc's crash (lap 3 to 4), Russell switched from medium to hard and Antonelli from hard to medium. Antonelli stopped again under the lap-28 VSC and ran the last 25 laps on new mediums. Russell never stopped again and finished on a hard tyre with 50 laps on it.

The gap went from 14.3 s on lap 29 to 0.25 s on lap 48, and Antonelli made the pass for good on lap 50.

Tyre availability: the option was lost on Saturday

The weekend allocation was 2 hard, 3 medium and 8 soft sets, plus one extra soft for reaching Q3. Russell had opened all three mediums before the start: one in FP1, one in Q1 and one for the race start. Antonelli ran all of qualifying on softs and kept two mediums.

So the scenario changed from "same tyre as Antonelli" to two options: a medium with 3 to 5 laps of use, or Russell's last new soft. New-tyre status comes from FastF1's inference, so this finding is rated medium confidence.

What a double stack costs the second car

On lap 27 Russell led with Antonelli 0.6 s behind, so Russell would have stopped first and Antonelli would have waited. I measured the cost on two reference races with paradas_duplas.py:

  • Australia 2026, lap 12 under VSC: Mercedes stacked both cars and the second car lost about 3 s. This is the reference number.
  • China 2026, lap 10 under Safety Car: Russell lifted off (0% throttle against 60% for Antonelli) to open space before the pit entry. The pit lane itself cost only 0.3 s extra; the loss moved to the in-lap, about 6 s.
Race simulation and the overtaking rule

The simulated Russell runs on Antonelli's real lap times, which already include the traffic Antonelli met. The overtaking rule only acts when a simulated car catches a real car it never met in the actual race: minimum gap 0.5 s, a window to attempt the pass, and at least 0.2 s per lap of pace advantage.

To calibrate the window I replayed Antonelli's real race through the model. A 1.5 s window is the smallest that reproduces his result exactly; at 1.0 s the model traps him behind Hamilton, whom he passed in reality. A binary rule creates steps, and a car stuck in traffic stops responding to its inputs, so both limits go into the report.

Provisional answer and rejection criterion

A first estimate says yes: about 0.8 s per lap of tyre advantage over 24 laps is roughly 19 s, against about 13.7 s lost in the stop, a margin near 5 s.

I reject the hypothesis if Russell finishes behind any car in the central scenario. The final answer will be a range ("Russell wins in X of Y plausible scenarios") instead of a yes or no.
